Studio Project Reflection

How the Final Design Meets My Initial Judgement of Purpose

My initial purpose for Bubble Bloom was to create a highly interactive and enjoyable digital tool that felt colourful, playful, and easy to use. I wanted each user action to produce immediate feedback, both visually and sonically, so that the process of making an image would feel lively rather than static. I was also interested in creating a low-pressure experience, where users could make an artwork without needing detailed drawing skills.

The final design reflects several important parts of this initial purpose, especially in terms of colour, feedback, and ease of interaction. Users can choose different shapes and colours, place them on the canvas, hold to grow them, and click again to burst them into smaller pieces. These interactions, together with animation and sound feedback, create a tool that is visually colourful and clearly interactive. The option to switch between light and dark canvas backgrounds, and the ability to save and download the final image, also support the idea of personal and playful image making.

However, I do not think the final design fully achieved the experience I originally wanted. While it successfully created colour, movement, and feedback, it was less successful in creating a deeper sense of enjoyment and stress relief that inspired the project. The interaction became repetitive more quickly than I expected, and the results sometimes felt too limited to sustain a strong sense of discovery. Through this, I learned that a playful tool is not defined only by bright visuals or responsive effects, but by whether the interaction can remain varied, readable, and satisfying over time.

How the Values Identified in My Initial Judgement of Worth Are Embodied in My Final Design

The main values I wanted Bubble Bloom to embody were playfulness, low-pressure creativity, and immediate feedback. These values were important to me because I did not want the project to feel like a technical drawing tool. Instead, I wanted it to feel approachable, enjoyable, and expressive, even for users without strong drawing skills.

Playfulness was most clearly embodied in the project’s visual language. The bright colour palette, simple shapes, burst animation, and sound effects all contributed to a colourful and active interface. However, reflecting on the final design, I now think that playfulness was embodied more strongly at the surface level than at the interaction level. At first, I understood playfulness mainly as a visual quality expressed through colour, cuteness, and surface style, but I learned that in an interactive tool, playfulness also depends on variation, unpredictability, and the feeling that interaction itself remains enjoyable over time.

Low-pressure creativity was embodied through the simplicity of the controls. Users can place, grow, and burst shapes without needing detailed drawing skill, and they can save and download their artwork afterwards. This supported my aim of making image creation feel more accessible. At the same time, I learned that reducing complexity is not enough by itself. When the interaction feels repetitive or the results feel limited, a tool can still become flat rather than freeing. This helped me understand that low-pressure design also depends on the quality and variety of the experience.

Immediate feedback was one of the most successfully embodied values in the project. Visual change, sound, hover cues, and movement all helped make the interaction feel reactive and legible. Users could clearly see and hear that their actions had an effect. However, I also learned that feedback alone is not enough to sustain engagement. It helps users understand the system, but a stronger variation is needed to make that interaction remain meaningful.

Overall, this reflection showed me that the values I identified at the beginning were present in the final design, but not always at the depth I intended. Bubble Bloom looks playful, feels responsive, and lowers the barrier to creating images, but it also revealed to me that values are not expressed only through appearance. They need to be fully supported by the interaction itself.

How I Approached My Chosen Interaction Design Principles in a Novel Way

The interaction design principles I focused on in Bubble Bloom were feedback and mapping. At the beginning, I thought I was already using these two principles clearly, because every interaction had some kind of response. Placing, growing, and bursting shapes all had animation and sound, and Different shapes also had slightly different sound identities. At that stage, I felt that if each function had feedback and mapping, then the interaction design principles were already present in the project.

However, after reflecting on the outcome, I realised that I had applied these principles in a surface-level way. Feedback was there, but mostly in a functional sense. It helped users recognise that an action had happened, but it did not always make the experience feel more emotional, satisfying, or memorable. In the same way, mapping was present, but it was also quite direct and mechanical. Different shapes had different sounds, and hold to grow had a matching growth sound, but these relationships did not always deepen the interaction as much as I intended.

One more novel aspect of the project was that image-making was structured through stages of interaction rather than direct drawing. Instead of only placing a shape, the user could place it, hold to grow it, and then click again to burst it into smaller scattered pieces. This made a transformation part of the creative process itself, rather than just a secondary effect. I think this was one of the more successful parts of the interaction design, because it made the tool feel more active and distinct from a simple shape-placement tool.

At the same time, reflection showed me that adding more interaction does not automatically make the tool more playful or more successful. What was missing was a deeper use of feedback and mapping to shape the feeling of the experience. For example, feedback could have been more emotional rather than only functional. After a burst, the scattered droplets could also have produced softer trailing sounds, so the burst would feel more real and more extended instead of ending too quickly. Mapping could also have been pushed further. For example, placing shapes in different areas of the canvas could have changed the pitch of the sound, which would not only create a clearer relationship between action and result, but also encourage more curiosity and exploration.

From this experience, I learned that feedback and mapping are not meaningful simply because they exist in a system. They become meaningful when they shape mood, variation, and user engagement. If sound is added, it should not only confirm that an action has happened. It should contribute to the feeling of the interaction itself. This reflection helped me understand that stronger interaction design is not just about making a system responsive, but about making that responsiveness feel purposeful and enjoyable over time.

Documentation and Discussion of Design Judgment Through Exploration

Design Judgment 1: Improving Burst Discoverability

One design judgment came from user feedback. During testing, some users did not realise that they could click a placed shape again to burst it. This showed me that the burst interaction was not discoverable enough. I explored several options to make this clearer, including adding a stronger hover effect, using an outer stroke, and testing a growing visual cue.

In the end, I chose to use stroke and hover as the main solution. This was the most suitable option because the buttons in the tool already used stroke, so it matched the existing design language and felt visually consistent. This decision was partly successful, because it made the interface clearer without changing the overall style too much. From this, I learned that discoverability is not only about making an effect more obvious. It also depends on whether the cue feels natural within the whole visual system.

Design Judgment 2: Choosing Light and Dark Canvas Modes

Another design judgment involved the canvas design. Earlier in development, I explored several different background styles, including grid-like and notebook-style canvases, because I wanted the tool to feel like a place for making images. However, after testing these directions, I realised that they did not work especially well with the colourful palette of the tool.

I then shifted toward offering light and dark canvas modes instead. This decision was more successful because it allowed the same colours and shapes to create different moods. On a light canvas, the result felt brighter and more playful, while on a dark canvas, the colours appeared more vivid and dramatic. This helped me understand that the background is not only a visual setting, but part of the emotional tone of the tool. I learned that small design changes can strongly affect how users read the same interaction and image differently.

Design Judgment 3: Refining Sound Design

The third design judgement was about sound design. At the beginning, I wanted the sounds to be short, sharp, and more directly connected to the bursting action. My intention was to make the interaction feel quick and satisfying. However, after testing this direction, I found that the result sounded unpleasant and too similar to a basic mouse click. It created functional feedback, but it did not add much character to the experience.

I then explored a different direction by adding more reverb and allowing the sounds to feel slightly longer and softer. This decision was more successful because it made the sounds feel richer and more recognisable, rather than purely mechanical. It also helped the interaction feel more textured and less abrupt. From this process, I learned that sound design is not only about confirming that an action has happened. It also shapes the mood and identity of the interaction, and can make the tool feel either flat or memorable.

My Finalised Project Production Timeline and How I Navigated It

In the early stage of Bubble Bloom, I already had a concept and general direction, so the project quickly moved from idea development into implementation. At first, I was mainly thinking about what functions the tool needed, what visual style it should have, and how to make it feel more like a complete tool rather than just a small interaction. The early prototype only allowed users to place a shape and then click it again to burst it.

After the peer feedback session, I realised that the first version felt too repetitive, and some users did not even realise that they could click again to trigger the burst. This feedback changed my priorities. Instead of continuing to add more decorative elements, I decided to focus on variation and interaction clarity. That was when I spent more time developing hold to grow, because I felt it could add more interest in a way that still matched the playful direction of the project better than a traditional size slider.

I also chose to prioritise sound refinement, because I felt the interaction still lacked texture. I did not want the tool to rely only on visual change, so I explored sounds that could make the grow and burst actions feel more distinctive. However, by the final stage of the project, I realised that I had navigated the process with too much focus on implementation. I was adding functions and solving coding problems, but I was not always asking whether those changes were improving the experience itself.

Through this process, I learned that navigating a design project is not only about following a schedule or adding more features after feedback. It also means knowing what to prioritise, what to leave out, and when to shift attention back from technical problem-solving to the interaction itself. In the end, the most important lesson for me was that a project can become more functional without necessarily becoming more meaningful and managing that difference is part of the design process.

The Aspect of Design I Am Most Proud of

The aspect of Bubble Bloom I am most proud of is that it allows users to create their own colourful artwork through very simple interaction. Users do not need detailed drawing skill or technical knowledge. By placing, growing, and bursting shapes, they can gradually create an image that feels personal and expressive.

I am most proud of this because it reflects one of my original intentions for the project. I did not want Bubble Bloom to feel like a tool that required precision or artistic training. Instead, I wanted it to feel more approachable and low-pressure, so that users could focus on interaction and experimentation rather than correctness. In particular, the burst interaction helped turn simple placement into a more active and transformable process, which made the artwork feel less fixed and more playful. The combination of simple shapes, colourful visuals, and transformation made it possible for users to create something that still looked like an artwork, even though the system itself was quite simple.

Although I became critical of some parts of the final project, I still think this aspect was successful. It showed me that even very simple interactions can support creativity when they are clear and visually rewarding. This is the part of the project that still feels most aligned with my original design intention, and it is the part that I would most want to keep and develop further in the future.

The Aspect of Design I Found Most Challenging

One of the most challenging aspects of Bubble Bloom was technical performance. The project was designed to let users keep placing shapes, bursting them, and gradually creating their own colourful artwork. This was also connected to the idea of stress relief, because repeated interaction and visual accumulation were meant to feel satisfying. However, when too many shapes were placed on the canvas, the system became noticeably slower. This reduced the pleasure of using the tool, because once the interaction lost smoothness, it also lost some of its intended relaxing and creative quality. At the time, I was not able to solve this fully, but after the project I understood that Konva allows different layers to be separated. If completed shapes are moved to another layer while new shapes continue to be placed on the active layer, the performance can be improved significantly. This made me realise that technical structure is not separate from user experience. It directly affects whether the concept can actually work in practice.

Another major challenge was my understanding of playfulness. At first, I thought playfulness mainly came from bright colours, cute sounds, childish elements, and simple interaction. In that sense, I believed that a colourful visual style and a bubble-bursting idea were already enough to make the project feel playful. However, after seeing other projects and reflecting on my own, I realised that this understanding was too shallow. Playfulness in an interactive tool is not only about surface appearance. It also depends on whether the interaction itself feels varied, responsive, unpredictable, and enjoyable over time.

These challenges taught me that design is not only about making something work or making it look appealing. It is also about shaping the kind of experience the user actually has. I learned that simple does not automatically mean low-pressure, colourful does not automatically mean playful, and technical completion does not automatically mean a successful design outcome. If I continue developing Bubble Bloom, I would focus both on improving technical performance and on making the interaction itself feel more satisfying, so that the intended experience is supported at both the functional and experiential level.

An Aspect of a Peer's Creative Tool that Provided Insightful Contrast to My Own Design Judgements

A peer project that gave me a useful contrast was Ripple. What stood out to me was that its playfulness did not come only from the visual style. The visual and interactive parts worked together very well, so the result felt stronger than either one on its own.

I was especially interested in how clear the concept and direction were. The project used an ocean theme, and the rocks were not just decoration. They also affected movement, collisions, and the combinations of sounds, which created more variation in the interaction. This made the project feel playful in a deeper way, because the playfulness came from the behaviour of the system, not only from how it looked.

In comparison, this helped me see that Bubble Bloom was simple, but not necessarily stress-relieving or meaningfully playful. I wanted the project to feel playful, but too much of that stayed at the level of colour and surface appearance. Ripple helped me realise that stronger playful design happens when concept, interaction, and visual language all support each other, rather than when playfulness is added mainly through style.
